About

I'm Tabitha.

I design customer-centred systems that help people succeed.

I was most recently Head of Learner Success at AltSchool Africa, leading the Community and Customer Support teams. Over five years there I worked across Customer Success, Community, Customer Experience and Learner Success, supporting more than 35,000 learners in over 100 countries while helping the organisation build better customer journeys. Alongside that I held a contract role with Google as Crowdsource Community Coordinator, driving adoption and contributions in the app and launching the Crowdsource learning programme across 30+ universities in six East African countries, work that drove more than 2 million contributions.

What I carry from one role to the next is a capability rather than a department. I can work out how people experience an organisation, find where that experience breaks, and build the systems that help them succeed inside it. The job titles have changed. That has not.

I am at my most useful where something is being built or fixed rather than maintained, in companies growing fast enough that the systems have not caught up with the customers yet.

Alongside all of it I have stayed active in the Kenyan tech community, volunteering as an organiser with GDG Nairobi since 2020. That work is running events for the developer ecosystem: planning them, hosting them, and making sure the people who show up leave with something. It is where a lot of my instincts about experience were formed, and I have no intention of stopping.

Outside work I enjoy writing, mentoring and speaking about leadership, customer experience and purposeful work.

Transformation One

From Reactive Support to Learner Success

Reimagining Customer Support as a strategic source of learner insight

Support was resolving issues well, but the conversations happening there were telling us something bigger. Learners were repeatedly saying where they got stuck and what confused them. I rebuilt the function so it could resolve problems and help the organisation understand why those problems kept happening.

21% reduction in response time 35,000+ learners 11 cohorts, 100 countries

Context

When I became Head of Learner Success at AltSchool Africa, Customer Support was performing an essential operational function: responding to learner questions and resolving issues as they arose.

As the organisation scaled, I saw an opportunity to make better use of the conversations already happening in Support. Learners were repeatedly telling us where they were getting stuck, what was confusing them, and where our processes were creating unnecessary friction.

The challenge was not simply responding faster. It was creating a support operation that could resolve learner problems well while also helping the organisation understand why those problems were happening.

Approach

I started by strengthening the way the Support function operated: documenting standard operating procedures, giving the team clearer context on the learner journey, and creating structured escalation pathways with Programme Managers and Academic teams.

I also asked the team to look beyond individual tickets. Recurring questions, repeated points of confusion, and common learner blockers could tell us something about the wider experience. I documented those patterns and surfaced them to the teams that could act on them.

Outcome

A more consistent and proactive support operation, with clearer escalation paths and better visibility into recurring learner challenges. Average human response time improved from 45 minutes 39 seconds to 36 minutes 03 seconds within one month, roughly 21%. More importantly, learner insights began informing operational decisions beyond the Support team itself.

Proof

  • Reduced average human response time by approximately 21%
  • Supported more than 35,000 learners across 11 cohorts from about 100 countries
  • Established documented support processes adopted across the Learner Success function
  • Learner feedback and recurring support themes informed operational improvements and cross-functional conversations

I don't see Customer Support as a function that simply closes tickets. I see it as one of the richest places to understand how customers experience an organisation, and one of the places where small operational changes create a better experience at scale.

Transformation Two

Designing Customer-Centred Retention at Scale

Connecting business goals, learner behaviour and proactive intervention

Retention was the problem I kept returning to. It started with payment reminders, became a conversation about what was actually blocking learners, and ended as an automated system that used LMS activity data to reach people before they disappeared.

Activation model adopted across cohorts Automated with Engineering

Context

One of the challenges I was trying to solve at AltSchool Africa was retention. As I thought more about the role Community could play, I became increasingly intentional about understanding the business and finding ways for Community to contribute to business goals while still creating value for learners.

Revenue was one of the metrics that mattered, which led me to think about tuition payments differently. I introduced payment reminders as one way to contribute, but I also knew what happened when a learner fell behind: some would eventually ask to defer or wait for another intake and start again.

So the payment reminder became more than a payment reminder. I used the communication to encourage learners to stay on track with their learning as well. Some appreciated the nudge. Others explained they were struggling financially or had become too busy to keep up. Those conversations reinforced something important: a learner not paying or progressing was not always a sign of unwillingness. Sometimes there was a blocker underneath it that we could only understand once we reached out.

Approach

I started looking beyond Community activity as a signal of learner progress. We had an LMS, which meant there was data showing which learners were engaging with their materials and which were not.

I reached out to the Data team to understand what was available and what we could track, then used that data to identify learners who had stopped engaging and reached out to encourage them back. At first I did this manually. The responses helped us separate learners who were simply busy and needed a nudge from those facing bigger blockers.

The manual process was useful for testing the approach, but it was not going to scale. I approached Engineering about automating the intervention, creating the learner segments and the messages for each group. Learners inactive for around two weeks received encouragement to get back on track; those inactive for longer received a more direct check-in focused on understanding what was getting in the way.

Outcome

The payment activation approach became repeatable across cohorts, and the LMS-based intervention created a more proactive response to inactivity. The important change was not sending more messages. It was moving from waiting for learners to tell us they were struggling to using available signals to start the conversation earlier.

Proof

  • Designed a payment activation approach adopted across subsequent cohorts
  • Supported learner success initiatives across 35,000+ learners and 11 cohorts
  • Used LMS activity data to identify learners no longer engaging with their materials
  • Worked with Engineering to automate segmented learner interventions based on inactivity

I try to connect the work my team owns to what the organisation is trying to achieve. I also look beyond the most visible metric for signals that tell me whether the people we serve are actually progressing. When a manual intervention works, I look for the system that makes it repeatable.

Transformation Three

Turning Customer Feedback into Organisational Strategy

Building a Voice of Customer system that turns listening into action

Feedback existed across surveys, support conversations, community discussions, check-ins and student leaders, but individual pieces stayed inside the channel that received them. I built the process that found the patterns and made sure they reached the people who could act.

15+ improvements influenced Insight across seven teams

Context

Customer feedback existed across surveys, Support conversations, community discussions, learner check-ins, student leaders and programme reviews.

The information was valuable, but it was easy for individual pieces of feedback to remain within the team or channel where they were received. I wanted to make sure recurring learner problems were not simply recorded. They needed to reach the people who could actually do something about them.

Approach

I brought together feedback from the different learner touchpoints and looked for recurring patterns rather than treating every response as an isolated issue. That meant analysing semester surveys and check-ins alongside Support conversations, community discussions and other learner interactions. I documented the themes, highlighted what needed attention, and shared the insights with the relevant stakeholders.

The important part for me was closing the loop. I didn't want feedback to end with a report. I followed up with the teams involved and advocated for the learner perspective in conversations with Product, Learning, Operations, Engineering, Finance and Leadership.

Outcome

Learner insight became part of cross-functional conversations around product, curriculum, operations, communication and support. The process influenced more than 15 improvements. The value was not in the number of surveys completed; it was in what the organisation chose to do with what learners told us.

Proof

  • Built a structured Voice of Customer process spanning multiple learner touchpoints
  • Influenced 15+ product, curriculum and operational improvements
  • Connected learner insight across Community, Product, Learning, Operations, Engineering, Finance and Leadership
  • Created reporting and follow-up practices that kept learner issues visible beyond the team that first received the feedback

I don't think listening is measured by how much feedback an organisation collects. It is measured by what the organisation is willing to change because of what it heard.

How I Think

Better systems create better experiences.

Many problems organisations describe as people problems are actually system problems that people experience.

Early on at AltSchool Africa, when a learner went quiet, I went after that learner. Sent the message, made the call, brought them back. Then a few weeks later it would happen again with somebody else, at roughly the same point in the programme. Working through enough of those is what showed me the more useful question was not how to reach each person faster, but where in the journey people were disappearing in the first place. It was nearly always the same place.

So now when something goes wrong I do not ask who dropped it. I ask what made it so easy to drop. Support tickets do not pile up because a team stopped caring, they pile up because nobody ever built a path for the hard ones. People rarely stop paying for a service because they stopped wanting what they signed up for. Something in the experience made stopping feel reasonable, and there was nothing built to catch them.

Whatever room I am in, I end up being the one asking about the person who is not in it: the learner, the attendee, the community member, the customer. And the question I keep coming back to is the same one: what would make success easier for the people we serve?
